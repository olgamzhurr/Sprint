Guiding Philosophy

We don't just plan. We execute.

Every session produces something real — a validated problem, a filled-in canvas, a sent message, a live website. By Day 10, you won't have a plan. You'll have a business. The workbook is not for reading — it's for doing.

On the work session

This is not a workshop where you listen and do it later.

The live work session is where the real work happens — not at home, not before the next day. Whatever you build in the room is real. Whatever you leave unfinished is a gap you carry into the next session. The people around you are doing the same thing at the same time. Use that.

Before You Begin

The Founder Mindset

Starting a business is not just a business challenge — it's a psychological one. The frameworks in this workbook are important. But your relationship with fear, failure, and uncertainty will determine more than any framework.

Reality Check · Before You Begin

Business or Hobby?

Both are legitimate. But they're built differently, measured differently, and require entirely different levels of commitment. Knowing which one you're actually building protects you from the hardest kind of burnout — the one where the two got quietly confused.

HobbyBusiness
RevenueOptional — nice if it happensRequired — the measure of whether it works
CustomerAnyone who shows upSomeone specific you've chosen and committed to serve
ConsistencyWhen you feel like itWhen you don't
FeedbackWelcome but optionalMandatory — customers define your direction
Measure of successPersonal satisfactionValue created for others, reflected in revenue

"Every business starts from one of two places: survival — when you have nothing to lose and urgency drives you forward — or grounded commitment — when you've made a deliberate choice to show up, plan, and build with discipline before you have to. Both work. What doesn't work is starting from a vague wish. Know which one you are."

Imposter Syndrome

"Who am I to start a business?"

Every founder feels this. Every single one. The question isn't whether you're qualified — nobody is at the start. The question is whether you're curious, committed, and willing to learn faster than you fail. Qualifications follow execution, not the other way around.

The Perfectionism Trap

Done is better than perfect. Shipped beats polished.

Perfectionism is procrastination in disguise. Every day you spend perfecting something before showing it to a real customer is a day you're learning nothing. The feedback you get from one imperfect launch will teach you more than six months of polishing ever could.

Fear of Judgement

People are less focused on you than you think.

The fear of being seen, judged, or failing publicly is real — and it's the single biggest thing that stops people from launching. Here's the truth: most people are too focused on their own lives to scrutinize yours. The ones who do notice will respect you for trying.

Framework · Methodology Block 1 — Three Sources of Business Ideas

Where validated business ideas come from

Strong business ideas don't come from brainstorming sessions — they come from a close relationship with a real problem. The three most durable sources:

SourceWhat it looks likeWhy it worksExample
PainA problem you personally experience, repeatedlyYou are the customer. Deep, lived insight — not research.Stewart Butterfield couldn't manage team communication across email. He built Slack.
PassionA domain you're obsessed with and know unusually wellLong-term staying power. You'll keep going when it gets hard.Whitney Wolfe Herd knew the dating app world from the inside. She built Bumble with a clear differentiated view.
PatternA trend or gap you observe through your professional workUnique vantage point others without your background can't see.Melanie Perkins saw every school struggling to teach design software. She built Canva to make design accessible.

The three-question test for any problem

Before committing to a problem, ask: Is it frequent? (Does your customer experience this regularly, not once a year?) Is it intense? (Does it cost them real time, money, or frustration?) Are they already paying? (Even a bad, expensive workaround is proof of demand.) A problem that fails all three is a feature request. A problem that passes all three is a business.

Framework · Methodology Block 2 — The "Why Now?" Test

Timing is half the business

A good problem can exist for decades without becoming a business. What creates the window is a shift — something that makes the problem more urgent, more solvable, or more visible today than it was before. Four types of shifts create timing:

TriggerWhat changedExample
Technology shiftA new tool makes the solution possible or dramatically cheaper than beforeStripe (2010) — payment APIs became accessible to any developer for the first time, not just banks
Behaviour changeHow people live or work has shifted, creating a need that didn't exist at scale beforePeloton (2012) — smartphone habits normalised subscription entertainment, making in-home fitness viable
Market gapA category has grown but an underserved segment is being ignored by existing playersGlossier (2014) — social media created a new beauty consumer who wasn't being served by traditional brands
Regulatory or macro changeA law, crisis, or economic shift creates urgency or unlocks new accessZoom (accelerated 2020) — remote work mandates turned a nice-to-have into a critical necessity overnight
Example · What a Strong Problem Statement Looks Like No solution mentioned. Specific customer. Real cost. Existing (inadequate) workaround.

Local service business: "Yoga studio owners and independent fitness instructors managed class bookings through phone calls, paper sign-up sheets, and Facebook messages — with no way to take payment in advance, track waitlists, or send automated reminders. Students regularly no-showed without cancelling, leaving instructors with empty spots they could have filled and no reliable weekly income." → Became Mindbody.

Physical product: "Serious outdoor enthusiasts and campers found that every consumer cooler on the market failed to keep ice beyond 24 hours in real field conditions. The only products with genuine performance were built for commercial fishing operations — heavy, expensive, and never designed for recreational use. Nobody had built a rugged, properly insulated cooler for people who actually spent days in the field." → Became YETI.

B2B service: "Independent wedding planners and event coordinators spent more than a third of their working hours chasing vendor quotes, unsigned contracts, and late payments across email and spreadsheets. Every new event meant rebuilding the same manual process from scratch, with no way to track what had been sent, agreed, or paid — and mistakes cost them client relationships." → Became HoneyBook.

Notice: each one names a specific customer, a specific context, a real cost, and an existing workaround — across three completely different business types. No solution is mentioned in any of them.

Framework Introduction · CusDev vs Product Dev

The real distinction

These aren't two phases that happen sequentially. They're two orientations that need to run in parallel — and most founders do 90% Product Dev and 10% CusDev, when early on it should be the reverse.

Product Dev thinking

"I know what to build. Let me build it well and fast."

Output: features, code, design, product

Risk: building something technically excellent that nobody needs

CusDev thinking

"I have a hypothesis about who has a problem. Let me find out if I'm right before I build."

Output: insight, clarity, evidence, direction

Risk: taking too long to talk and never building anything

CusDev Win · Airbnb Understanding the customer before trusting the idea

Before Airbnb built anything serious, the founders flew to New York, knocked on hosts' doors, photographed apartments themselves, and stayed with early guests. They did the CusDev in person. What they discovered changed the product entirely: people weren't looking for a listing platform. They needed to feel safe. That insight — trust as the product, not accommodation — came from conversations, not from code. The photography programme, the reviews system, the entire brand direction: all of it came from what they learned by showing up.

The lesson: they could have built a listings site. Instead they built a trust machine. The difference was 20 conversations before 20,000 lines of code.

CusDev Skip · Google Glass Technically extraordinary. Socially untested.

Google Glass was an engineering triumph. The team assumed people would want a camera on their face in public — in restaurants, at the gym, in conversations. Nobody adequately tested that assumption with real people in real social contexts before building. The backlash was immediate and fatal: "Glassholes" became a cultural insult. The product wasn't rejected because it didn't work. It was rejected because wearing it made other people uncomfortable — something any honest customer conversation would have surfaced.

The lesson: your customer's social environment is part of the product. Conversations would have caught this before $1 billion in development.

The Exception · James Dyson

Dyson built 5,127 prototypes over 15 years. No customer interviews. He mortgaged his house, his wife taught art to keep them afloat, and he kept going until it worked. No market research. No pivot based on feedback. Pure product obsession — and it succeeded. Why? Because Dyson was his customer. He lived the frustration of a vacuum losing suction daily for years. He didn't need interviews because he had 15 years of being the customer before he had 15 years of building the prototype. The problem was physical and universal, the solution was objectively measurable, and the market was already proven — people were already buying vacuums.

Are you a Dyson?

Do you live this problem every day, with the same intensity he had? Do you have the financial foundation to keep going no matter what — for years — until the market comes around? If yes, Product Dev first may be your path. If not, your customer needs to do the guiding. That's what the next 30 minutes is about.

The Mom Test · Rob Fitzpatrick

Ask about the past. Ask about their life. Not your idea.

❌ Wrong✓ RightWhy
"Would you buy this?""Tell me about the last time this happened."Past behaviour is evidence. Future intent is flattery.
"Do you think this is a good idea?""What did you do when that came up?"What people do beats what they say they'd do.
"How much would you pay?""How much have you already spent trying to solve it?"Existing spend is proof. Imagined spend is noise.
"Would this solve your problem?""What have you already tried?"Workarounds reveal the real intensity of the problem.

Framework Introduction & Best Practices · Day 3

Five principles for designing an offer that works

An offer sells an outcome, not a description Dollar Shave Club sold convenience, not razors. Slack sold focus, not messaging. YETI sold performance in the field, not a cooler. Dyson sold clean floors, not vacuum technology. The mechanism is secondary — the change in the customer's life is the offer. Your VP Canvas from Exercise 1 is the answer: map it directly to what you say you sell.
One offer at launch. Always. WhatsApp launched with messaging only — no web, no video, no business features. Airbnb was one listing page. Innocent Smoothies started with 3 flavours at a music festival stall. Glossier launched with one serum. MUJI launched with one notebook. One clear offer converts better than a menu every time. If your launch offer needs a paragraph to explain, it is too complex.
The best launches cut the right things Twitter had no hashtags (users invented them), no DMs, no photo uploads. Amazon launched with search + buy + receive — nothing else. GoPro launched as one camera for one use case: surfers. Spanx was one product for 10 years before expanding. The Must Have list in your MoSCoW is not what you think is important — it is what your customer cannot pay without having.
Pricing is positioning — and must match your customer's reality Your price communicates your market position before anyone uses your product. But it must also reflect what your specific persona can and will pay. Your VP Canvas sets the ceiling: if the value you create does not justify the price, the customer walks. Look at your persona from Day 2 — what do they already spend on this problem? That is your pricing anchor. A price too high kills you before your first sale; too low signals low quality and attracts the wrong customer.
Launch → Improve → Expand — in this order Launch the minimum version that proves your core value. Then make it better — quality, less friction, features customers actually ask for. Then make it wider — new segments, new markets, new products. Reversing this order is the most common reason founders run out of money before finding what works.
